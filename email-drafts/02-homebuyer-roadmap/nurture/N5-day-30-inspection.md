```
Hey *|FIRST_NAME|*,

When you eventually get to Step 7 in the roadmap, the inspection is going to feel scarier than it should. Here is how to read it without panic.

The first reframe: an inspection report is not a pass or fail. It's a list of facts about the house. Your job is to sort the facts, not react to all of them.

Sort everything into two buckets.

Bucket one: cosmetic and normal wear. Hairline drywall cracks, an old water heater, a sticky window, minor caulk issues, paint that needs touching up. These appear on every report on every house. They are not deal-breakers. They are the cost of owning anything older than five years.

Bucket two: structural and expensive. Foundation movement, an HVAC at the end of its life, an outdated electrical panel, signs of long-term water intrusion, polybutylene plumbing, a failing main sewer line, a roof past 15 years. These are the items that decide whether you negotiate, ask for credit, or walk.

The question to ask on every finding:

Would I still buy this house if this item never got fixed?

If yes, don't fight it. If no, decide whether it's worth negotiating or whether the house itself is wrong for you.

Florida specifics worth knowing before your first offer.

The standard home inspection is separate from the 4-point inspection and the wind mitigation report. The 4-point covers roof, electrical, plumbing, and HVAC for insurance underwriting purposes. The wind mitigation documents what protective features the house has (hip roof, shutters, opening protection) and directly affects what your insurance premium will be. On older homes, carriers will often require both before they bind a policy.

Roof age is one of the biggest insurance variables in Florida. A roof past 15 years can trigger non-renewal letters, premium surcharges, or outright denials. If a house you're considering has a 16-year-old roof, that's not a minor finding. It's a number you build into your offer.

And the thing every report misses: inspectors can't see behind drywall, under slabs, or inside sealed systems. They can flag symptoms (stains, smells, performance issues). They can't open a wall. If a finding makes you want to know more, call in a specialist (plumber, electrician, structural engineer) for a follow-up during your due-diligence period. That's what the period is for.
```
