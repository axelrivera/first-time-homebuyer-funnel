# FTHB Roadmap - Day 37 - Objections

**Subject:** Wait for rates or buy now: the actual math

**Preview:** The two objections that cost first-time buyers more than the rate itself.

**Send:** Day 37 (7 days after N5)

---

```
Hi %contact_first_name%,

Two objections come up more than any others when a first-time buyer stalls. Both feel cautious. Both are usually wrong.

Objection one: I should wait for rates to drop.

Run the proportions.

As an illustration only: on a roughly $450K, 30-year fixed loan, a 1% rate drop works out to roughly $290 a month, or about 10% of your principal-and-interest payment. Your numbers will differ based on actual rate, loan amount, term, and how long you hold the loan; treat this as a sanity-check on the order of magnitude, not as a quote.

That sounds like a lot until you measure what waiting costs.

If you wait 18 months for rates to move, you spend 18 months of rent in the meantime. At Orlando market rents, that's tens of thousands of dollars paid to a landlord, building zero equity. The rate drop is a bet; the rent is a guarantee.

And refinancing exists. If you buy now and rates drop later, you refinance into the lower rate. You don't lose the house you wanted to wait for the rate on. The house you wanted is still yours.

The cleaner framing: marry the house, date the rate.

Objection two: I need to save more first.

Probably you don't. The 20% down number is a myth that costs first-time buyers years.

Real loan products close every day in Orlando with low single-digit percent down (FHA, Conventional 97, HomeReady, Home Possible). Yes, PMI adds to your monthly payment until you reach 20% equity. But you get to that 20% faster than most first-time buyers expect, because home value appreciation counts toward it, not just principal paydown.

Saving toward 20% down typically means three more years of rent (tens of thousands of dollars paid to a landlord) while you scrape together tens of thousands more in cash, with no guarantee the same house at the same price will be there at the end.

Here is the honest version of when waiting is the right call: your credit score is rebuildable and six months of work will move you into a meaningfully better rate tier, or your income situation is mid-change (new job, contract work that needs season-over-season verification). Those are real reasons.

Rates might drop and I need a bigger down payment usually aren't.

If something in your specific situation actually makes waiting the right call, the Buyer Strategy Session is the place to walk through it. More on that next week.

-%agent_first_name%
```
